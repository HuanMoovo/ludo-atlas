# Ludo Atlas · Indie Survival

> Positioning: the 44 profiles' experience in Indie Developer Profiles, turned into your own **income-and-expense sheet, path ladder and decision rules**. Indie development is not just "making games"; it is a set of methods for continuously running yourself.
> Companions: Production Handbook (how to finish) · Live-Ops & Growth (how to sell well) · Legal, Patents & Competition (how to sign the right contracts) · Resources (tools and ecosystem entry points).
> Disclaimer: this is a summary of experience and does not constitute financial or legal advice; for figures and policies, verify against the latest situation in your own location.

## 1. Survival Model Overview (Mode First, Ideals Second)

| Mode | Cash prerequisite | Time prerequisite | Main risk | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Part-time mode | A day job / stable income | 10–20 hours per week | Timeline stretches 2–4×; motivation drains | Developers making their first complete commercial work (the safest option) |
| B. Savings mode | A 12–24 month runway of living expenses | Full-time | Runway burns out before the work is done | People with a proven finishing record who can self-manage |
| C. Hybrid project mode | Contract work covers living costs | Full-time (time split in half) | The original project gets squeezed by client work | People whose skills monetize (programming/art/TA) |
| D. Ecosystem-support mode | Sponsorship / advances / grants | Full-time or part-time | Dependent on external rhythms and contract terms | Long-term projects (simulation/tool-oriented); people with an existing fan base |

> **Selection principle**: for a first commercial work, default to **A**; only consider B/D if you have shipped something with healthy numbers; C is "war feeding war" — keep an **inviolable time block** for the original project (such as every morning).

## 2. Finances: Runway and Budget (Run the Numbers First)

### 2.1 Runway Math (Savings Mode)

| Item | Calculation | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly expenses | Rent / food / commute / insurance / phone ≈ the real cost level in your city | Don't fool yourself with an "extreme frugality" version |
| Social insurance and income tax | Freelancers must pay these themselves (policies vary by region) | Ask your local social-insurance office in advance |
| Development costs / month | Tool subscriptions + outsourcing reserve + expo/travel | One-off hardware counted separately |
| Buffer | Total runway × 1.3 | Illness / accidents / overruns are the norm |
| **Runway (months)** | (total savings - buffer) ÷ monthly expenses | 12–24 months recommended; **under 9 months, full-time is not advisable** |

### 2.2 Project Budget Template (Pre-Launch)

| Category | Reference share | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outsourcing (art/music) | 20–40% | Decides how well it sells; the most worthwhile money you'll spend |
| Tools/software | 5–10% | Compressible with a good free stack (see §3) |
| Expos/events | 5–15% | Offline exposure, as needed |
| Store asset production | 5–10% | Capsules/trailers (the biggest lever in Live-Ops & Growth §2) |
| Contingency | 10–20% | Store review delays / hotfix needs |

### 2.3 Revenue-Expectation Discipline

- Zero revenue before launch is the norm; **the first month usually carries the bulk of the year's revenue** (Live-Ops & Growth §6), so don't budget on a "breakout hit" curve.
- Forecast in three tiers: floor (recoup your costs) / median (enough for the next title) / ideal (don't plan on it).
- When the first payment arrives, do three things first: set aside tax → top up the runway → seed money for the next title.

## 3. The Earn-More, Spend-Less Toolbox

- **Free development stack**: engines (Godot etc.), art (Krita/Blender/Pixelorama), audio (Audacity/LMMS), office tools (Feishu/Yuque); for the full list see Resources §1-§2.
- **Free assets first**: use free assets throughout prototyping (Kenney etc., §5); replace only the assets that are "confirmed to stay".
- **Outsourcing timing**: consider outsourcing when "your own quality/speed" becomes the bottleneck and asset volume exceeds 30 items (process in Production Handbook §4.3, Art & Audio Handbook §6).
- **AI savings and red lines**: AI pays off most for concepts/placeholders/batch assistance; for AI disclosure and copyright red lines on shipped assets, see design doc §14.9 and Legal, Patents & Competition. **Cost-cutting must not cross red lines.**
- **Time savings**: keep a fixed weekly "deliverable" cadence (Production Handbook §7) and bill yourself in "completion", not "hours worked".

## 4. The Path Ladder: From Jams to a Flagship Title

| Rung | Form | Timeline | Acceptance criteria | Usual outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Jam / toy | A small prototype (48 hours to 2 weeks) | Days / weeks | Run the full "release" loop once | You either get hooked or quit |
| 2 Free small game | A complete small game released for free | 1–2 months | Strangers finish it and leave a comment | 90% of people stop here (and that's perfectly fine) |
| 3 Commercial small game | A small piece priced at $1–5 | 2–6 months | Make your first sale; run the whole pipeline end to end | Confidence and process muscle |
| 4 Flagship title | Your "signature work" | 6–24 months | Recoup your costs + a word-of-mouth base | The decisive battle that sets your career direction |
| 5 Long-term / IP | Live-ops / sequels / series | Years | A stable audience and repeat purchases | Becoming a studio, or an IP |

- **Ladder discipline**: don't skip rungs (going straight to 4 is one of the biggest killers for beginners; see Pitfalls & Anti-patterns); each rung adds exactly **one** more variable over the last (release channel, pricing, community, outsourcing).
- Staying at 23 and shipping steadily is a perfectly respectable way to live. **Finishing matters more than fame.**

## 5. The Full Map of Funding Sources (Ordered by Accessibility)

| Source | Suitable stage | Key points | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Early access sales (Steam EA) | 34 | The most realistic "funding": let players' money feed development | Fits systems-driven games; use with care for narrative games |
| Savings / part-time income | All stages of A/B | The cleanest cash flow | Do the runway math (§2) |
| Contract work / commissions | Mode C | Programming/art skills monetize directly | Keep a fixed time block for the original work; don't take contracts that eat over 40% of your time |
| Crowdfunding (Kickstarter/Modian) | Early 4 | Needs a demo + video + community base; don't over-promise on rewards | Low success rates; heavy fulfillment pressure |
| Ongoing patronage (Patreon/Afdian) | 5 and long-term projects | Fits the "watchable long-term" model (Dwarf Fortress / Sokpop) | Requires a steady stream of visible output |
| Publisher advance | 34 | Traded for revenue share and terms; read the eight must-check clauses in Legal, Patents & Competition §5.1 first | Net-revenue definitions and IP ownership are the core of the negotiation |
| Platform support programs | 4 | Console platform incubation (e.g. ID@Xbox, Sony's China Hero Project), GWB and others | Limited slots; binds certain platform rights |
| Local industry support | 45 | Cultural-creative and game-industry policies in some cities | **Go by the latest local policy**; don't distort your project for a subsidy |

## 6. Publishing Decisions: Quick Reference

- **Self-publishing vs a publisher**: evaluation matrix = revenue share × advance × resources (media/expos/localization) × control (IP/sequels/pricing) × exit terms; when unsure, self-publish a small game first to practice the pipeline.
- **Platform mix**: PC (Steam as the base) → console (find a publishing partner / platform program) → mobile (the user-acquisition economy, see Live-Ops & Growth) → mini-games (domestic monetization, see Multi-platform Launch Playbook §6); don't spread your first title across every platform.
- **Demo strategy**: a Demo is a marketing asset, not charity (Live-Ops & Growth §2); before joining platform festivals (Next Fest and the like), leave ample debugging time.
- **Pricing**: benchmark against the pricing band for similar quality and genre (Legal, Patents & Competition §7.2-7.3); a 10–15% launch discount is standard; low price / high volume vs high price / premium are both strategies — don't end up stuck between the two.

## 7. Mindset and Sustainability (The Most Easily Underestimated Survival Item)

- **Family and social communication**: prepare a "one-pager" for family outside the industry: what I do, why, how long it will take, the financial bottom line, and the failure plan; **transparency kills 80% of the friction**.
- **Loneliness and burnout**: regular social contact (dev communities / game-dev buddies), a stable routine, and at least one day a week away from the project; if you find you "haven't wanted to open the project for two weeks straight", that is a mandatory-rest signal (Pitfalls & Anti-patterns §6).
- **The antidote to comparison**: what you see are survivors' breakouts; what you don't see is the silent denominator (Game History / Indie Developer Profiles only record the winners); measure your pace against your own previous version.
- **Positive-feedback design**: ritualize milestones (celebrate even a small release); keep a public devlog to collect "someone is watching" signals (Live-Ops & Growth §8).

## 8. Decision Checkpoints: Continue / Pivot / Cut

| Signal | Threshold reference (set your own) | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timeline over budget | > 50% | Trigger the feature-cut contingency (Production Handbook §3); re-estimate the remaining schedule |
| External playtests | Two rounds in a row with nobody wanting to keep playing | Go back to the prototype layer: fix the core loop or swap the mechanic |
| Interest exhausted | Four weeks straight with no drive | Shelve it or shrink the scope; rest first, then decide |
| Financial warning | Less than 6 months of runway left | Switch to Mode C (take contract work) or go part-time |
| Sunk-cost trap | "I've been at it two years" is the only reason left | Re-evaluate it as a new project: would you green-light it? |

- **"Cutting" done right**: archive it (code / assets / postmortem), make an announcement (if it was public), and reuse the assets. **There are no wasted projects, only failures without a postmortem** (Production Handbook §9).
- **Pivoting is not failure**: shrinking your flagship title to a 3-scale scope and finishing on time is a mature judgment, not a retreat.

## 9. Special: Indie Development in Mainland China

- **The commercialization reality**: official domestic channels (app stores / mini-game payments / domestic PC platforms) require a game license; so the mainstream route is **international PC platforms as the main line + mini-game ad monetization as a supplement + multilingual overseas releases** (details in Legal, Patents & Competition §7 and Multi-platform Launch Playbook §7).
- **Ecosystem entry points**: the GWB incubator/awards, the indiePlay competition, CiGA events, WePlay Expo, TapTap's indie-friendly policies, bilibili publishing — taking part = exposure + connections + feedback in one (entry points in Resources §4.2, §7.2).
- **Communities**: indienova, CowLevel, the bilibili dev zone, various QQ/WeChat groups — finding 5–10 fellow travelers matters more than anything.
- **Costs and cities**: living costs vary widely; for savings-mode runway math, always use local figures (§2.1).
- **Policy**: some cities have cultural-creative / game-industry support and park policies; go by the latest word from the competent authorities, and don't distort your project for a subsidy.

## 10. One-Page Action Checklist (Start Today)

1. Write down your **mode choice** (A/B/C/D from §1) and your reasons.
2. Run the runway math: how many months can you burn? (§2.1)
3. Pick a 3-scale project (not the dream project) and write a one-pager (Production Handbook §1).
4. Set a weekly cadence: goals on Monday, a build on Friday (Production Handbook §4).
5. Set up a public presence (one is enough) and start a devlog (Live-Ops & Growth §8).
6. Check in monthly: budget sheet, risk sheet, mental state (§7/§8).
7. At every milestone, send a playable build to strangers (not friends).
8. In the three months before launch, run the Multi-platform Launch Playbook + Live-Ops & Growth as checklists, item by item.
9. Do a postmortem 90 days after release; whatever the outcome, write the postmortem, settle the books, and decide on the next title.
10. Remember what history keeps proving (Game History §6): **a finished small game always beats an unfinished masterpiece.**
