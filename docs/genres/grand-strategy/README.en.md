# Ludo Atlas · Genre Handbooks · Grand Strategy

> **Genre Handbooks · Volume 4**. Positioning: the strategy genre whose first-order content is running a nation through history. Players read the board, set strategy and push forward in pausable, continuous time, while politics, economy, military and diplomacy feed one another as stacked systems; the map and opening are mostly given, historical scenarios supply the stage, exploration and expansion take supporting roles, and working the situation you inherit takes center stage.

## 1. Positioning and Core Loop

In one line: grand strategy is the genre that **turns "one nation's history" into a simulation**. Players take over a situation with a past: fixed geography, fixed neighbors, fixed means. The task is not to invent a civilization but to read the situation and, over the span of generations, push it toward where they want it; battles can be delegated to generals, decisions cannot.

The core loop, written as a verb chain:

`read the board (map, ledgers, characters, the diplomatic board) → set strategy (alliances, war, reform, marriage) → dispatch executors (armies, envoys, spies) → time advances (pausable) → the world rebounds (war reports, revolts, succession, market swings) → reassess, and back to the next round`

The loop is measured in months and years: one time advance is a small commitment, a stretch of national policy a long-term bet. The structural difference from turn-based games is the time model: continuously flowing, pausable, adjustable in speed; pausing is this genre's default reading posture, not an advanced feature.

Drawing boundaries against neighboring genres:

| Neighboring genre | Boundary |
| --- | --- |
| 4X | 4X starts from zero with exploration and expansion as its main axis; grand strategy thins out that loop: the map and opening are fixed, genuine unclaimed land is rare, and the center of gravity shifts to historical simulation and role-play — what you manage is a nation and a web of relationships that already exist |
| RTS | Also real-time, but pausable and without an action ceiling; what's tested is decisions and coordination, not hand speed |
| Wargame | A wargame runs a military simulation of one war or campaign; grand strategy puts the military inside the larger board of politics, economy and diplomacy, where war is only one instrument of several |
| Management sim | A management sim watches income, spending and production curves; grand strategy's opponents are living historical powers, and win or loss is written in the fate of nations, not only in the spreadsheets |

A self-check question: take away map generation and expanding from zero — does the loop still turn? If it does, your players care about "where this slice of history goes", and you are making a grand strategy game; if it doesn't, go back to 4X.

## 2. Player Experience Goals and Benchmark Titles

Experience goals (in priority order):

1. **A sense of historical presence**: the opening is not a blank map but a stage with a past; every decision can be checked against real history, and "what would I choose if this were real?" is a pleasure players bring with them.
2. **Systems-driven storytelling**: when a campaign ends, the player can tell the story of how a nation arrived at today — a story generated by the systems and written by the player's own hand; characters and families are its threads.
3. **Control at a slow pace**: information and planning buy a sense of control, and time pauses or accelerates on demand; read the situation correctly and the game is half won.
4. **Long-horizon plans that pay off**: plans spanning generations eventually land, and the gap of delayed gratification stretches longer than in 4X.
5. **"You can do that?" discoveries**: coupled systems grow tricks in players' hands that no tutorial ever wrote down (marrying into an inheritance claim, blockading a neighbor into collapse); the discovery itself is content.

Benchmark titles (widely known works only; play each to mid-campaign before breaking it down):

| Title | What to learn from it |
| --- | --- |
| Crusader Kings III | The character layer: making "people" the interface into the systems; the density of event narrative; succession, factions and schemes generating story on their own |
| Europa Universalis IV | The state layer: the diplomatic board, trade and colonization, national policy managed across centuries; small events stringing together grand history |
| Hearts of Iron IV | The war machine: industrial mobilization, production lines and battle plans, turning a world war into a nationwide stress test |
| Victoria 3 | Economy and politics: population, markets and reform interlocking, pulling a nation's arc from agrarian society to industrial power |
| Stellaris | A bridge between a grand strategy skeleton and a 4X opening: an exploration start, event chains and galactic politics |
| Total War: Three Kingdoms | A different architectural route: a grand-strategy campaign layer spliced with real-time battles |

There is a single unified question when breaking these titles down: how do they keep players wanting to do things in their hundredth hour?

## 3. Design Essentials

### 3.1 Nested Systems: Four Systems Feeding Each Other

Grand strategy stands on coupling: politics, economy, military and diplomacy are not four parallel panels but one machine feeding itself; any single one is bland on its own — only meshed together do they hold up.

| System | What the player does | Where the output goes | A typical loop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Politics | Appointments, reform, suppressing factions | Stability, legitimacy, manpower | Stability feeds taxes and war endurance |
| Economy | Taxation, trade, construction | Money and grain, production capacity, reinforcements | Money feeds the army, diplomacy and public works |
| Military | Raising troops, marching, fighting | Territory, deterrence, casualties | The cost of war eats back into money, grain and stability |
| Diplomacy | Marriage, alliances, threats, betrayal | Security, treaties, inheritance claims | Strength feeds leverage; isolation limits your options |

Design discipline:

- **Every system outputs to at least two and takes input from at least two**: a system that only takes input is decoration; one that only sends output is a cheat.
- **Snowballs need brakes**: the rich getting stronger must meet natural resistance (overextension, war weariness, rebellion, succession crises), or the outcome locks in at mid-game and the remaining dozens of hours are all going through the motions.
- **Coupling must be visible**: panels spell out where each number comes from and where it goes (§3.5); invisible coupling does not exist.
- Tuning discipline: tune systems apart, test them together; a single system's numbers looking good means nothing — what matters is whether it stays under control after a dozen-odd hours of simulation.

### 3.2 Time and Pacing: Continuous Time, Monthly Resolution

Time typically has two tiers: the day is the resolution substrate (marching, construction sites, sieges), the month is the ledger the player faces (taxes, supply, the event pool), and the year sets the beat (technology, reform, character growth); the smallest increment the player sees is the month, and the interface always carries a line for "today's date".

- **Pause is the reading posture**: speed settings, auto-pause on events, auto-slowdown when war breaks out — all configurable, so players have time to finish reading before deciding.
- **Pacing is tuned through event density**: peacetime uses low-frequency, heavy events to maintain presence; wartime is handed to the systems' own high-frequency feedback.
- **Campaign length must be designed**: a complete campaign starts at dozens of hours and marathon runs reach into the hundreds; the standard is not total length but "every hour still holds new decisions", delivered by handing out new verbs in each era rather than stacking the same choices bigger.

### 3.3 Events and Scripting: Historical Hooks plus Conditional Triggers

The event system is the narrative engine and the king of content value-for-money: a block of text plus a few numbers gives abstract systems concrete, memorable moments. Events fall into four kinds by duty, made separately:

| Type | Trigger | Duty | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Historical hooks | Time, place or character conditions | Anchors that keep history moving along its script | A war breaks out somewhere in a given year; a monarch takes the throne |
| Conditional events | State thresholds or weighted randomness | Let the systems grow stories of their own | Grain prices spike, the eve of a revolt, a disputed succession |
| Decisions and missions | Triggered by the player | Fold long-term goals into actionable checklists | Reform, moving the capital, establishing a state religion |
| Flavor events | Low frequency, light effects | Keep the world's texture and human touch alive | Court gossip, local color |

- **Triggers must be readable**: when an event fires, the player should be able to answer "why now, why me"; behavior that can't be explained gets filed as a bug.
- **Events must write back into the systems**: an event that only pops text without changing state is a billboard; every one should move at least a number, add a status or flip a switch.
- **Keep two pipelines separate**: historical main-line events guarantee the narrative arc; the random pool creates variance. Main-line events carry one-shot flags; pool events carry weights and cooldowns.
- **Scripts should read like content, not code**: a declarative script with trigger conditions and effects layered apart lets a designer finish an event on their own (§4); text is delivered in a fixed structure of title, body, options and consequences.
- **The character layer is capacity permitting**: characters are heavy content (portraits, traits, event adaptation); if capacity falls short, retreat to "a nation plus a head of state"; if capacity allows, a set of succession and relationship rules is the best event generator available, cheaper than hand-writing a hundred story events.

### 3.4 Opponent AI: Believable Beats Clever

Grand strategy AI is a famously hard problem: it has to play monarch, advisor, treasurer and diplomat at once, while a human player runs all those departments with one brain. Set the goal as **readable, counterable and characterful** — don't chase "human-like":

- **One rules interface, many parameter tables**: the AI goes through the same rules entry points as the player, with only public difficulty parameters added; different factions use different personality presets (aggressive, mercantile, vengeful, conservative), which is cheaper and more controllable than writing several algorithms.
- **Decisions must be explainable**: attitude lists, casus belli and threat assessments are all laid out on the interface; the first time a player can't understand AI behavior, every AI action afterward is suspect.
- **Tune difficulty in the open**: adjust resources and speed, and be extremely restrained about quietly rigging information; get caught doing it once and trust is gone.
- **Layer the AI and lower its frequency**: the strategic layer recalculates rarely (ambitions, alliances), the campaign layer once every few years (who to attack next), the tactical layer in every engagement (how to fight this battle); the higher the frequency, the cheaper each thinking pass must be.
- **Use AI to validate systems**: batch AI-vs-AI runs are the only realistic way to answer "will this system run away, lock up or degenerate"; log behavior from day one.

### 3.5 UI and Information Presentation: Managing the Readability of Complexity

The UI's job is not to fill the screen with numbers but to press complexity into a readable shape; this section is a design question, engineering is in §4.

- **Overview plus drill-down**: one entry point for "what most deserves attention right now" (alerts, opportunities, lapsed treaties), with clicks drilling layer by layer down to causes; information is tiered by "does this need handling now?", not stacked by system category.
- **The map is the first interface**: factions, supply, terrain and reinforcements are drawn in layers on the map, each layer a different view; tables are for verification, the map is for intuition.
- **Tooltips nest**: from a number to its formula, then to the sources of its modifiers; heavy players can verify to their heart's content, light players skip at a glance.
- **Flatten the opening**: a suggestion system, objective cards and beginner-friendly factions press the first hour's barrier down to where players can get in the door; complexity is the selling point, it cannot be the entry fee.

## 4. Technical Essentials

The engineering thesis in one line: **the technical challenge is not real-time performance but the scale of the simulation, the AI's budget and the scripting of content**. What follows is engine-agnostic; details are in the Programming Handbook.

- **Simulation core**: fixed-timestep resolution, with the system resolution order written into the spec and kept stable; change the order and saves and war reports stop matching. Decouple logic from presentation: the UI reads resolution snapshots, and staging can be delayed, sped up or skipped.
- **Simulation granularity**: draw levels of abstraction — use statistics where statistics belong (populations and markets as queues and distributions), keep names where names matter (monarchs, ministers, generals); reserve individual-level simulation for entities that affect the narrative, and set granularity and performance budget together.
- **Determinism**: randomness runs on a reproducible seed system, and the call order of logic randomness is fixed; replays, batch simulation and debugging all depend on it, and "same save, same result" is the foundation of balance work.
- **Data- and script-driven**: provinces, factions, events and decisions are all externalized as data and scripts, and code only reads tables; the script engine offers two declaration layers, triggers and effects, with a validator for references and conflicts; mods and official content share the same layer, and hot reload lets content edits skip the compiler.
- **AI budget**: layered calls with their own budgets, amortized across frames or run asynchronously; when over budget, lower frequency and intelligence rather than stutter. AI state must serialize into saves.
- **Balancing tools**: telemetry (national development curves and outcome distributions), a hidden debug panel and batch simulation (hundreds of AI-vs-AI campaigns) are the only realistic means of tuning balance; manual tests find problems, batch runs verify fixes.
- **UI engineering**: tooltips generated from data (number plus formula plus sources, nestable), virtualization for long lists (hundreds of provinces, over a thousand characters), layered map rendering and switching; all text is externalized, and localization word budgets are a scheduling item (§5).
- **Late-game performance**: entities grow as the campaign runs, and per-tick cost rises with them; use incremental updates and dirty flags, drop the precision of distant secondary systems, and amortize the most expensive pathfinding and evaluations. Write "the simulation-time ceiling after year X" into the spec — don't wait for player complaints.
- **Saves and versions**: in a campaign that runs dozens of hours, corrupted saves are the heaviest class of bad review; saves carry a version number, migration functions and rotating backups; modded saves record script versions; run old saves through before every update.

## 5. Content Volume and Workload Reference

The following are typical magnitudes for projects of this kind, for scope estimation only — not a commitment.

| Project form | Content scale | Time reference | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Systems prototype | 1 regional map, 2–4 factions | 2–3 months | Zero art; validates monthly resolution, diplomacy and a minimal AI |
| Single-scenario title | 1 scenario, dozens of factions, hundreds of events | 1–2 years | Events and UI are the invisible bulk of the work |
| Multi-scenario scale | 3–5 scenarios, a hundred-plus factions, over a thousand events | 3+ years | The starting scale for matching classic series |
| Long-term live ops | Multiple scenarios plus a content update pipeline | 5+ years | Content ships monthly; longevity comes from iteration |

The cost structure of content units:

| Content unit | Unit cost (magnitude) | Starting volume | Full scope | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Event | Half a day to 2 days each | 50–150 | Hundreds to thousands | Including text, scripting and testing |
| Faction | 1–3 days each (numeric profile) | 4–6 | Dozens to a hundred-plus | Rule-layer differences priced separately |
| Scenario | 1–2 months each | 1 | 3–5 | Map slicing, layout and historical fact-checking |
| Province | Data priced by the hour; terrain art tallied separately | 50–100 | Hundreds to thousands | Count times unit cost — the source of all scale problems |
| Characters (optional) | Mostly systems building; low per-entry cost | Optional | Depends on your team | Portraits and animation are the big art items |

Two conclusions of scale: AI and balance are the invisible bulk — reserve over 20% of the total schedule for "tuning AI and balance", and have batch simulation tools from the prototype stage (§4); individual events are cheap but the total is scary, and a player reads fewer than half the events in stock across one campaign — lay the main-line hooks correctly first, then fill the pool. Scope-cutting priority: scenario count, faction count, character-layer depth; AI and the event system rank last — cut them and the genre stops existing.

## 6. How to Start the First Prototype

A first prototype runs 2–3 months: one small regional map, 2–4 factions, zero art, validating one thing only — whether monthly resolution plus diplomacy plus an AI that does things on its own can hook a person.

1. **Map and provinces**: 50–100 provinces, each with three values — manpower, taxes and supply; four playable factions plus a handful of small neighbors.
2. **Time and resolution**: continuous time with monthly resolution and two or three speed settings; pausing lets you read full-screen information.
3. **A minimal system loop**: taxes and manpower support troop raising; marching and fighting can take land; add one diplomatic action (alliance or marriage, choose one); close one coupling loop (war drains stability; stability moves taxes).
4. **Events and hooks**: 30–50 event scripts, 5–10 of them condition-triggered (succession, rebellion, border friction), validating the validator along the way.
5. **A rough AI**: assesses strength, declares war and sues for peace, grabs easy gains; behavior logging starts on day one.
6. **An end condition**: a decidable ending (survive to a given year or achieve a set goal), so a campaign has a finish line.

Acceptance (all observable):

- With art and audio stripped out, leaving only the map, numbers and text, testers are still willing to play for over an hour straight.
- Find 3–5 people who have never played it: unaided, within half an hour they can state "the three things I'm most worried about right now" and "how I plan to survive".
- When the player does nothing, the AI keeps the situation moving (alliances, wars, land changing hands) without freezing or standing still.
- For every event that fires, the tester can answer "why now".

Anti-examples: building the full map first, the complete event library first, portrait art first, multiplayer first. Until the system loop stands up, all of it is sunk cost.

## 7. Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Symptom | Avoidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI left to the finish line | The content is done but the AI is still a wooden post; the game doesn't stand up | AI is half the gameplay — schedule it at greenlight and have a minimal version in the prototype (§3.4) |
| Systems that sit side by side without meshing | Several panels each doing their own thing; playing any of them any way works | At least two ins and two outs per system (§3.1); whatever coupling you write, test it |
| Snowballs with no brakes | The mid-game leader locks in victory; the remaining dozens of hours go through the motions | Make overextension, war weariness, rebellion and succession crises real mechanics |
| Events that only pop up windows | You read the text and nothing changes, or the change is invisible | Every event writes back at least one visible state (§3.3) |
| No new problems in the mid-to-late game | Old choices scale up on repeat; decision density bottoms out | Hand out new verbs each era; build the closing clock in from the design stage (§3.2) |
| Late-game performance collapse | The longer you play, the slower it gets and the bigger the saves roll | Budgets, incremental updates, reduced-precision abstraction (§4) |
| Corrupted saves | One update, one broken save; "dozens of hours down the drain" | Version migration, rotating backups, mod flags (§4) |
| Rigging information to make difficulty | The AI sees through the fog, gets caught, and every action afterward is suspect | Difficulty through public parameters; cheating kept to an extreme minimum (§3.4) |
| Treating the log as the interface | All the information is there; players can't find it | Overview plus drill-down, nested tooltips (§3.5) |
| Scope out of control | The map keeps growing and factions keep multiplying | Cut scenarios, cut factions, cut the character layer; AI depth ranks last (§5) |
