# Ludo Atlas · Game History (Field History · Technology History · Notable Works)

> Positioning: a quick tour of "where we come from": a brief history of video games, a brief history of Chinese games, a brief history of game technology, and the representative works of each era.
> Conventions: years refer to **first release / publication** (regional differences exist); notable works are selected for "industry impact and pioneering status"; facts follow public sources, and a few date details differ between accounts (controversial phrasings have been avoided where possible).

## 2. The Six Eras in Detail, with Notable Works

### 2.1 The Pioneering Era (1958–1977)

Oscilloscopes in laboratories and university computer rooms gave birth to interactive entertainment: from tennis simulations to space battles, from coin-operated arcades to the first home console. The legacy of this era: **a game is first of all an interactive rule system**.

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tennis for Two | 1958 | Widely recognized as one of the earliest interactive video games (oscilloscope tennis) |
| Spacewar! | 1962 | One of the first video games with lasting influence; a direct inspiration for later arcade ventures |
| Computer Space | 1971 | The first commercial coin-operated arcade machine (Nolan Bushnell and Nutting) |
| Pong | 1972 | The starting point of video game commercialization (Atari) |
| Magnavox Odyssey | 1972 | The first home video game console |
| Colossal Cave Adventure | 1976/77 | The ancestor of text adventures; established the interactive tradition of narrative through exploration |

### 2.2 The Arcade Golden Age and the Home Console Revolution (1978–1990)

Arcades became a center of popular culture; Japanese companies rose; after the home console market went through the 1983 video game crash, the FC/NES rebuilt order, and **content quality and platform licensing** became cornerstones of the industry from then on.

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space Invaders | 1978 | The start of the arcade peak; set the Japanese market alight |
| Asteroids | 1979 | A vector-display classic |
| Pac-Man | 1980 | The first mass-market game icon; a pioneer of marketing and licensing |
| Donkey Kong | 1981 | The prototype of platforming; the game that made Shigeru Miyamoto's name |
| Dragon's Lair | 1983 | Laser-disc animated arcade game; an early taste of the cinematic turn |
| Tetris | 1984 | Simple rules × endless depth; a legend that crossed political blocs |
| Super Mario Bros. | 1985 | Defined the platformer; a "game design textbook" |
| The Legend of Zelda (original) | 1986 | The beginning of exploratory adventure and save-game logic |
| Video game crash of 1983 | 1983 | The industry lesson: content flooding = market collapse (see §6) |
| Game Boy + Tetris | 1989 | Handheld + casual, a one-two punch that took video games worldwide |

### 2.3 The 16-bit Era and the Rise of the PC (1990–1995)

Console performance doubled and genres matured across the board; the PC found its own path with RTS, simulation and FPS; the age-rating system was born amid controversy.

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sonic the Hedgehog | 1991 | The mascot war of the console battles; design built on a sense of speed |
| Street Fighter II | 1991 | The game that defined fighting games; arcade versus culture |
| Mortal Kombat | 1992 | The direct catalyst for age ratings (ESRB, 1994) |
| SimCity | 1989 | The foundation of the systems-simulation category |
| Civilization (original) | 1991 | The original recipe for 4X and "one more turn" |
| DOOM | 1993 | The FPS paradigm established + shareware distribution + mod culture |
| Myst | 1993 | Riding the CD-ROM capacity boom; exploration-driven narrative |
| Super Metroid | 1994 | Established the Metroidvania template |
| Chrono Trigger | 1995 | The narrative benchmark of the JRPG golden age |

### 2.4 The 3D Revolution and the Dawn of Online (1995–2003)

From polygon consoles to 3D accelerator cards, the perspective revolution rebuilt almost every genre; engines began to be licensed and reused; online play moved from LAN to the internet and MMOs first sprouted.

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Super Mario 64 | 1996 | The control and camera paradigm for 3D platforming |
| Final Fantasy VII | 1997 | JRPG globalization + cinematic storytelling + the CG industry |
| StarCraft | 1998 | The competitive peak of RTS; the start of Korea's esports industry |
| Half-Life | 1998 | The seamless-narrative FPS; a mod ecosystem (CS grew out of it) |
| Metal Gear Solid | 1998 | Cinematic stealth; a benchmark for narrative presentation |
| The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time | 1998 | The lock-on camera benchmark for 3D adventure |
| StarCraft / Brood War, Diablo II | 1998/2000 | The loot loop and the Battle.net ecosystem |
| Shenmue | 1999 | An early open-world prototype (and an equally classic business lesson) |
| The Sims | 2000 | Mass-market simulation; "digital dollhouse" design |
| GTA III | 2001 | The open-world city paradigm |
| Halo: Combat Evolved | 2001 | The game-feel standard for console FPS + the prelude to Xbox Live |
| Counter-Strike | 2000 | The model case of mod → commercial product |

Digital distribution reshaped channels; MMOs and MOBAs defined "games as a service"; motion controls opened up casual users; the iPhone began the mobile-game era; indie games grew from a crack into a forest.

| FarmVille | 2009 | The prototype of social games and F2P live-ops |
| Minecraft | 2009–2011 | Sandbox creation + the early-access model + a global phenomenon |
| Braid | 2008 | The coming-of-age of indie games; auteur design |
| Super Meat Boy / The Binding of Isaac | 2010–2011 | Hardcore indie + the roguelike wave |

Phones became the largest game platform; indies went premium-quality (Hades / Disco Elysium / Balatro); battle royale and social play rotated through; CRPGs revived; Chinese companies entered the global top tier; AI production tools disrupted the whole industry.

## 3. A Brief History of Chinese Games

| Stage | Period | Marker | Representative works / events |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awakening | 1980s–1990s | Arcades, Subor learning computers, Taiwanese developers | Softstar's The Legend of Sword and Fairy (1995); Soft-World, Dynasty International and others |
| Single-player and setbacks | Late 1990s | Mainland studios begin self-developing single-player games | Seasun's Jian Xia Qing Yuan (1997); Blood Lion (1997), a widely discussed failure case |
| Online PC games | 2001–2010 | Licensed and self-developed MMOs boom | The Legend of Mir 2 (2001, Shanda); NetEase's Westward Journey Online and Fantasy Westward Journey; Perfect World; Zhengtu (2006, Giant) |
| Browser games & social | 2008–2013 | Browser-game user acquisition and social games | The 4399 ecosystem; QQ Farm (2009) |
| Mobile era | 2013–2019 | Smartphone adoption and channel-driven growth | Soul Hunters (2014, Lilith Games); Honor of Kings (2015); Onmyoji (2016); Arknights (2019) |
| Quality & going global | 2017–2021 | Premium production + globalization + the indie rise | Genshin Impact (2020); Sky: Children of the Light (2019, Jenova Chen); The Scroll of Taiwu (2018); Dyson Sphere Program (2021); Tale of Immortal (2021) |
| Contemporary | 2022–present | AAA breakthrough, the mini-game ecosystem, AI adoption | Black Myth: Wukong (2024); the WeChat mini-game ecosystem; Zenless Zone Zero (2024) |
| Regulatory milestones | 2000s–present | Key points that set the industry's rhythm | The online-game regulatory system takes shape; the 2018 suspension and resumption of game-approval licences; the 2021 anti-addiction rules (playtime / spending limits); see the Multi-platform Launch Playbook §7 |

> Threads that characterize Chinese games: **fast channel turnover** (online PC → browser → mobile → mini-games), **a strong live-ops drive** (user acquisition, co-publishing, social virality), and **going global as a second growth curve**; indie developers, for their part, built a stable path through the Steam/WeGame ecosystem after 2017.

## 4. A Brief History of Game Technology

### 4.1 Hardware and Displays

| Period | Technology | Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1970s | Vector displays (Asteroids) | Crisp lines, but solid scenes could not be drawn |
| 1978+ | Raster displays + hardware sprites | Sprites + scrolling become the language of 2D |
| 1980s | Sound cards and dedicated sound chips (PSG/FM) | Music becomes a hardware capability (SID, YM series) |
| 1990s | CD-ROM, SGI workstations, 3D accelerator cards (Voodoo and others) | A double explosion of capacity and 3D compute |
| 2000s | Programmable-shader GPUs (from the GeForce 3 generation) | Rendering moves from the "fixed pipeline" to programmer-defined freedom |
| 2010s | Ray-tracing hardware (RTX, 2018), mobile SoC performance soars | Cinematic lighting in real time; mobile visuals approach console quality |
| 2020s | Ray tracing goes mainstream, cloud rendering and streaming, WebGPU ships | Rendering is everywhere; platform boundaries blur |

### 4.2 The Graphics and Rendering Line

2D sprites and tiles → pseudo-3D (ray casting and BSP in Wolfenstein 3D / DOOM) → polygon rasterization (manual sorting on the PS1, which had no Z-buffer → a hardware Z-buffer on the N64) → texture mapping and lightmaps → programmable shaders (GLSL/HLSL) → physically based rendering (PBR) → global illumination (baked lightmaps → real-time GI) → ray tracing and denoising (DLSS/FSR-class upscaling) → pipelines built into modern engines (UE5 Lumen/Nanite-class technology turns "high-end rendering" into a default capability).

### 4.3 The Evolution of Engines and Tools

- **The in-house era** (1980s–1990s): every company reinvented the wheel; the engine was an internal asset.
- **Engines become commercial products** (from the late 1990s): the Quake/Unreal licensing model turned "the engine" into a business; middleware such as RenderWare served a large number of studios.
- **Engine democratization** (2005–2015): Unity (2005) lowered the barrier; UE4 switched to free + royalty (2014); open-source engines such as Godot (from 2014) offered a third path.
- **Today**: generational updates in UE5 (2022) and Godot 4 (2023); in-house engines are concentrated at the largest studios and for special needs; **"engine choice" becomes a project-level strategy** (see the Programming Handbook §8 comparison table).
- Toolchains evolved in step: level editors went from hard-coded → general-purpose editors (the Tiled/LDtk ecosystem) → level tools built into engines; art tools went from pixel editors → a fully open-source Blender pipeline.

### 4.4 A Brief History of Sound Technology

Buzzers and square waves (late 1970s) → programmable sound chips and FM synthesis (1980s) → PCM sampling and CD-quality audio (1990s) → streamed compressed audio (2000s) → audio middleware (FMOD/Wwise-class tools that turn mixing, parameters and adaptivity into systems) → spatial audio and dynamic mixing (today; see the Art & Audio Handbook §7–§9).

### 4.5 A Brief History of Input Devices

Dials and buttons (the first arcade machines) → joystick + multiple buttons (the arcade golden age) → the D-pad (1980s, Nintendo sets the standard) → analog stick + rumble (the N64/PS1 generation) → touchscreens (from 2007, with touch-native play) → motion control and cameras (the Wii/Kinect generation) → touchpads / back buttons / adaptive triggers (current consoles) → VR controllers and hand tracking. **Revolutions in input are usually accompanied by a wave of new genres** (motion control for party games, touchscreens for casual mobile games).

### 4.6 A Brief History of Networking Technology

LAN multiplayer (early 1990s) → internet play (QuakeWorld and others reduced perceived latency; Blizzard's Battle.net) → MMOs and persistent worlds (1997–2004) → hybrid client–server and P2P architectures → prediction/rollback/lag compensation in modern competitive games → cloud gaming and streaming (experimental) → multiplayer as a service (managed backends, matchmaking services). For a deep dive, see the Programming Handbook §5.

### 4.7 The Evolution of Distribution and Business Models

Arcade coins → cartridge buy-to-own (1980s) → PC boxed copies / shareware (1990s) → online distribution and the internet-café ecosystem (a China-specific pattern) → digital stores (2003+) → F2P + in-app purchases (2008+, social/mobile) → battle passes and seasons (2010s) → subscriptions (Game Pass and the like) → indie subscription crowdfunding (the Patreon model, e.g. Dwarf Fortress) → ad monetization in mini-games (the WeChat/Douyin ecosystem). **Every channel shift reshuffles the industry's ranking.**

### 4.8 Game AI and the Contemporary Frontier

Scripted AI (pathfinding/state machines, from the 1980s) → behavior trees and GOAP (2000s) → machine-learning assistance (DDA, matchmaking, anti-cheat in the 2010s) → generative AI enters production (from 2022: images/audio/code/text) → AI NPCs and runtime generation (experimental) → coding agents and engine MCP (workflow-level impact). For compliance and disclosure, see the the AI Workflows Handbook §8.

## 6. Lessons from History (for Today's Developers)

1. **Periods of platform transition are windows for newcomers**: arcade → home console → PC → mobile → mini-games; every shift crowned a new king, and the next window sits somewhere between AI and XR.
2. **Quality control is the floor under a platform's prosperity**: the direct lesson of the 1983 video game crash — without a content-review system, the market drowns in low-quality products (compare the crackdowns on manipulated rankings in today's stores).
3. **Technology revolutions first change what can be done, then how it is done**: raster displays, 3D, networking, mobile, AI — each was a tool first, and new genres and business models second.
4. **The long tail and sustainability depend on ecosystems**: Steam / indie publishers / crowdfunding / the mini-game ecosystem prove that "small teams can be sustainable" is an institutional product, not luck.
5. **Waves of imitation follow originals by 2–3 years**: many chase the trend, few succeed; combinatorial innovation and vertical depth have repeatedly proven the safer bet (see the Legal, Patents & Competition handbook §7).
6. **The distinctiveness of the Chinese path**: channel- and live-ops-driven, with rapid iteration; the way out lies in premium and niche titles that do not depend on a single channel.
7. **Preserving history is accumulating method**: each era's failure modes recur in new skins; half the pitfalls in Pitfalls & Anti-patterns are history homework.
