# Ludo Atlas · Case Studies

> Selected industry case studies with ample public information, each organized in four parts: background, key practices, results, and reusable conclusions.
> Figures follow official announcements and mainstream reporting; wording is held to a "publicly verifiable" standard, with no extrapolation. Selection principle: prefer fewer, better cases — lessons matter more than stories.
> Companions: Game History (era context) · Pitfalls & Anti-patterns (point-by-point comparison) · Live-Ops & Growth (the data view).

## 1. Long-Run Successes (indie titles)

### Stardew Valley (Eric Barone)

- **Background**: One-person development; originally a practice project; over four years in the making.
- **Approach**: Art, music, programming and writing all done alone; audience built in public early on; steady large free updates after launch.
- **Results**: 400,000 copies in the first two weeks; cumulative sales announced past 30 million in 2024; a perennial top seller in the farming-sim category.
- **Takeaway**: Unmet demand (the Story of Seasons line had gone dormant) + one person covering every role + long-term updates — three things stacked into this result. Low price with high value density is a repeatable strategy; covering every role alone is a personal choice.

### Hollow Knight (Team Cherry)

- **Background**: A three-person core team in Australia, starting with a 2014 crowdfunding campaign.
- **Approach**: A volume of content far beyond the team's size; hand-drawn art unified to the extreme; free DLC after launch as a gift back to players.
- **Results**: The contemporary benchmark of the Metroidvania genre; sales in the tens of millions.
- **Takeaway**: A small team trading a small budget for a long production runway is a workable path. The price is sequel-expectation management (the long wait for Hollow Knight: Silksong) — a lesson both the team and the players are learning.

### Hades (Supergiant Games)

- **Background**: A mid-sized indie team; roguelike plus narrative.
- **Approach**: Early access first (late 2018), polished with over a year of player data; tied "die and retry" to narrative progression so that even failure produces story.
- **Results**: After the 1.0 release in 2020, acclaim and awards together; the standard example of "polishing in early access".
- **Takeaway**: Early access suits systems-driven games. Once the fusion point between narrative and repeated play is found, a roguelike is no longer "repetitive grind".

### Celeste (Extremely OK Games)

- **Background**: Expanded from a prototype made at a four-day game jam; a two-person core team.
- **Approach**: Kept the prototype's core game feel and expanded it over two years; shipped with an Assist Mode at launch, covering both difficulty and accessibility.
- **Results**: Released in 2018; synonymous with high-difficulty platforming, and a positive example of accessibility design.
- **Takeaway**: Validating the core with a jam prototype before deciding whether to expand into a full game is the cheapest risk control. Difficulty design does not have to exclude players — give them options.

### Vampire Survivors (poncle)

- **Background**: One-person development by a developer with a mobile-game background; priced under three dollars.
- **Approach**: Stripped out aiming and complex controls, keeping only the satisfaction of "growth and the horde"; high-frequency updates during early access.
- **Results**: Broke out in 2022 through streaming, opening up the "survivors-like" genre.
- **Takeaway**: Genre innovation often comes from cross-pollinating experience across platforms. Subtraction tests judgment more than addition does.

### Balatro (LocalThunk)

- **Background**: An anonymous developer, building the game over several years in spare time.
- **Approach**: Poker rules plus roguelike numbers; few systems, but deep; stayed low-key during development and let the finished game speak.
- **Results**: A phenomenon-level hit among 2024 indie games, winning multiple year-end awards.
- **Takeaway**: The depth of a system combination is itself the selling point; no persona or marketing story is needed to pave the way.

## 2. Business Models and Platforms

### Minecraft (Mojang)

- **Background**: Started from a prototype shared on forums; went on sale in 2009.
- **Approach**: The pioneering practice of paid early access — selling while building; community feedback went straight into the development loop.
- **Results**: Version 1.0 in 2011; Mojang sold to Microsoft for $2.5 billion in 2014; still one of the best-selling games of all time.
- **Takeaway**: Early access is not only a funding mechanism; it is a co-development mechanism. Platform thinking (letting players create content) pushed it beyond being a game.

### Fortnite (Epic Games)

- **Background**: Launched in 2017, catching the battle-royale wave.
- **Approach**: Free-to-play with in-app purchases and full cross-platform play; later turned the game into an events platform (concerts, crossovers, creator tools).
- **Results**: Became a cultural phenomenon and Epic's cash engine, driving the Unreal Engine and store ecosystem.
- **Takeaway**: When a product becomes a "place", its life cycle is decided not by version updates but by community activity.

### Sheep a Sheep (WeChat mini-game)

- **Background**: A tile-matching mini-game that flooded the WeChat mini-game platform in September 2022.
- **Approach**: An extremely low entry barrier and an extremely hard second level, manufactured to drive sharing and discussion; monetized with ads.
- **Results**: Discussed across the whole internet within days; a landmark case of social virality in mini-games, accompanied by controversy over gameplay and ad experience.
- **Takeaway**: The spread logic of the mini-game ecosystem is completely different from PC — social virality is the main engine. But traffic won by "designed controversy" does not retain people; after the second level, content still has to carry the game.

## 3. Failures and Crises

### Atari and E.T. (1983)

- **Background**: In the later Atari era, inferior cartridges flooded the market and retailer confidence collapsed.
- **Approach (what went wrong)**: Rushed production and mass distribution, with quality control absent.
- **Results**: The industry crashed; large numbers of cartridges were buried (confirmed by the 2014 excavation); the North American console market was crippled for years.
- **Takeaway**: Content flooding in with no quality gatekeeping — the market corrects itself with a crash. Compare with today's storefront crackdowns on review manipulation: the logic has not changed.

### Blood Lion (Shangyang, 1997)

- **Background**: The hype peak of China's early domestic single-player era; expectations were pushed extremely high.
- **Approach (what went wrong)**: Marketing far exceeded the actual state of completion; the shipped version was plagued with problems.
- **Results**: A long-discussed failure in Chinese game history, which also damaged players' trust in domestic single-player games at the time.
- **Takeaway**: One gap between promise and delivery is enough to ruin a reputation and the market that follows. The lesson still applies today.

### Anthem (BioWare, 2019)

- **Background**: A major project in which a well-known studio pivoted to games-as-a-service; direction swung back and forth during development.
- **Approach (what went wrong)**: According to in-depth reporting by several outlets, the project went a long time without a unified vision, and key decisions were repeatedly overturned.
- **Results**: Content was thin after the 2019 launch; in 2021 the studio officially abandoned the 2.0 plan.
- **Takeaway**: Vision drift is the chronic disease of large projects. For a live-service game, launch is only the starting point; teams without a content pipeline should not take on this model.

### Cyberpunk 2077 (CD Projekt, 2020)

- **Background**: A high-expectation AAA production; released in December 2020.
- **Approach (what went wrong)**: Shipped the last-generation console versions far below the quality bar; treated the release date as an immovable target.
- **Results**: Pulled from console storefronts for months amid a refund scandal; years of fixes followed, alongside a 2022 animated series and the 2023 version 2.0 update and expansion, and its reputation recovered.
- **Takeaway**: The release date is a business decision; quality is a survival decision — when they conflict, the latter wins. Repair is possible, but far costlier than a delay.

### Concord (Sony, 2024)

- **Background**: Sony's competitive shooter in development for years; launched in August 2024.
- **Approach (what went wrong)**: Blurred market positioning, head-on competition with established rivals, and a paid buy-to-play entry into a competitive genre where free dominates.
- **Results**: Servers shut down and refunds issued about two weeks after launch; the studio closed afterwards.
- **Takeaway**: A competitive product must be either free or have a distinctive selling point; with neither, years of development cannot save a two-week launch. Competitor analysis (Legal, Patents & Competition §7) is the money-saving step.

## 4. Redemption

### No Man's Sky (Hello Games)

- **Background**: Huge promises before the 2016 release; the gap between the launch version and expectations triggered a PR crisis.
- **Approach**: No defensiveness, just continuous free updates. Over the years, release after release filled in the originally promised features one by one and added new content such as multiplayer.
- **Results**: Its reputation turned around year after year, becoming the industry's model of "long-term repair"; the team grew steadily from a dozen-odd people.
- **Takeaway**: Promise management is the lifeline; once it fails, the only path is continuous delivery. This repair path requires cash flow and psychological resilience — not every team can afford to take it.

### Final Fantasy XIV (Square Enix)

- **Background**: The 1.0 version launched in 2010 collapsed in reputation.
- **Approach**: Brought in Naoki Yoshida to completely rebuild the team; rewrote the architecture, running the old version while building 2.0; in 2013, A Realm Reborn launched, writing the "destruction" of the old world into the new story.
- **Results**: Became a top global MMO, thriving for over a decade.
- **Takeaway**: A collapsed product can be saved, provided the team admits the problem, changes the blood and gives it enough time. Writing the "reboot" into the narrative itself is the most elegant move in this case.

### Cyberpunk 2077 (the follow-up arc)

- The B side of the §3 case: the animated series drove the recovery, version 2.0 reworked the systems, and the expansion sold well. Every element of a repair was in place: fix seriously, fix continuously, fix with momentum.

## 5. Case Studies from China

### The Scroll of Taiwu (ConchShip Games)

- **Background**: A wuxia sandbox of the complex-systems school, developed by a small team.
- **Approach**: Launched into early access and bound the update cadence to player suggestions; dug deep into systems, not visuals.
- **Results**: Early access opened in September 2018; over 800,000 copies in the first month and past one million in under three months (official announcement); long-term updates ever since.
- **Takeaway**: The Chinese-language market has real demand for "deep systems". Early access is the only realistic development path for complex systems.

### Dyson Sphere Program (Youthcat Studio)

- **Background**: A small team of a few people in Chongqing; the factory-automation genre.
- **Approach**: Chose a genre with no competition at the time and delivered on engineering quality and optimization; released globally.
- **Results**: Launched in January 2021; over 350,000 copies in the first week and over 1.5 million after seven months (public reporting); a benchmark for Chinese indies going global.
- **Takeaway**: A genre vacuum plus engineering quality — even a small team in a small city can win worldwide.

### Black Myth: Wukong (Game Science)

- **Background**: A Chinese single-player project pushing for AAA scale; released in August 2024.
- **Approach**: Made Chinese cultural subject matter the core selling point, launched simultaneously across platforms, and ran a complete global marketing cadence.
- **Results**: Within days of release, all-platform sales passed 10 million per official announcements, with a Steam concurrent peak above two million (public data).
- **Takeaway**: A distinctive subject can be a global competitive advantage. Its most valuable message: Chinese subject matter has a global market — that matters more than the level of the graphics.

### Chinese Parents (indie team)

- **Background**: A raising simulator about Chinese family education; launched in 2018.
- **Approach**: Turned collective memory into gameplay and let talkability drive the spread; restrained in scope.
- **Results**: Discussion across the internet far exceeded its development scale; a representative subject-driven success.
- **Takeaway**: Subject resonance is the cheapest distribution engine, but its boundaries and sensitivities need careful handling.

### Genshin Impact (miHoYo)

- **Background**: Launched in September 2020 with a cross-platform release.
- **Approach**: Industrialized content (an extremely steady version cadence), free-to-play with in-app purchases, globally synchronized operations, multilingual localization.
- **Results**: Long ranked near the top of global mobile-game revenue charts; a landmark case of Chinese games going overseas.
- **Takeaway**: A content cadence stable enough to be predictable is the core capability of global long-term operations — far more important than any single marketing push.

## 7. How to Use These Case Studies

1. At kickoff, check your project against the four traits of "long-run success" and ask how many you have.
2. At every milestone, tick off the "failure patterns" — especially promise management and release-date discipline.
3. When a crisis hits, read the redemption cases and first work out whether cash flow can sustain a repair path.
4. After the case studies, read the primary sources: official announcements, developer interviews, GDC talks (entry points in Resources §3).
